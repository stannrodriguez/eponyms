## Origin

G. K. Chesterton put it in The Thing in 1929, in a chapter on why he would not join reformers who wanted institutions cleared away. He describes a fence across a road and a modern type who says he sees no use in it and proposes to clear it away. Chesterton's answer is procedural: go away and think until you can come back and tell me what it is for; then I may allow you to destroy it.

## Mechanism

A working safeguard hides the problem it was built for. The fence keeps the cattle off the road, so nobody alive has seen cattle on the road, so the fence looks unnecessary — the absence of the hazard is evidence the fence works, not evidence it is idle. Rules also outlive the people who wrote down the reason, which means the reason is usually unrecorded rather than nonexistent.

## Where it breaks

It governs the order of operations, not the verdict — the fence may well come down once you know what it was for. Used as a general veto it is unfalsifiable, because an unknown reason can always be asserted and never disproved. Investigation is also not free: where the fence has an ongoing cost and its origin is genuinely unrecoverable, insisting on the reason first means removing nothing, ever. The rule earns its keep where removal is irreversible and delay is cheap.

## Example

China's Four Pests campaign in 1958 targeted sparrows as grain thieves, and citizens were mobilized to kill them by the hundreds of millions. Sparrows also ate crop-damaging insects, so removing them disrupted the ecological balance and worsened agricultural problems. This was one contributing factor within a famine driven by much larger policy, production and distribution failures. The campaign still illustrates the fence: the birds' protective function was easy to overlook because the problem they suppressed was less visible while they were there.
