## Origin

Roy Amara ran the Institute for the Future, the Palo Alto forecasting group spun out of RAND, from 1971 to 1990. He stated the line in conversation and in talks rather than in a paper, and it circulated among forecasters for years before it was written down and named.

## Mechanism

The two halves have different causes. In the short run, the demo is the cheap part: a working capability still needs infrastructure, regulation, trained users and rebuilt workflows, and those move on the timescale of institutions rather than of engineering. In the long run, the same technology becomes a component other people build on, and the recombinations compound — which is a curve people forecast badly because they extrapolate from the rate they can currently see.

## Where it breaks

As stated it cannot be wrong, which is its main weakness. With no timescale attached, any forecasting error can be sorted into short-run overestimation or long-run underestimation once the outcome is known. It also only describes technologies that arrive: the ones that were overestimated and then never delivered are simply overestimated, and the law offers no way to tell those apart from the slow arrivals while it still matters.

## Example

A working draft of the human genome was announced in 2000 with predictions of imminent cures, and the Human Genome Project was completed in 2003. By the mid-2000s the verdict was that genomics had not delivered. Meanwhile sequencing a genome fell from roughly 100 million dollars in 2001 to under a thousand by the mid-2010s, and tumour sequencing, prenatal screening and pathogen tracking became routine clinical work. The early disappointment and the later transformation came from judging the same technology on different timescales.
