## Origin

Laurence J. Peter was a professor of education who wrote the book with the playwright Raymond Hull; The Peter Principle appeared in 1969 and sold in the millions. It is written as satire — mock case studies, invented jargon — but the central claim is empirical and Peter meant it.

## Mechanism

Promotion is a reward for performance in the current job, and it is paid in a different job. The two jobs need different skills: the best engineer's advantage does not transfer to managing engineers. Someone who performs well keeps being moved until they reach a role they perform badly, at which point the promotions stop and they stay. A 2018 study of American sales organizations found exactly this pattern — sales performance strongly predicted promotion to management and predicted worse performance once there.

## Where it breaks

It assumes promotion is paid for performance in the current role and that skills do not transfer, and it treats the ceiling as permanent — real organizations demote, coach and fire, all of which drain the accumulation. Where promotion is decided on tested aptitude for the next job rather than results in the present one, the mechanism does not run at all. It is a claim about a matching process, not about the people caught in it.

## Example

A 2019 study of 53,035 sales workers across 214 firms found that a worker's own sales record strongly predicted being promoted to sales manager — and predicted, after promotion, worse performance from the team they went on to manage. The firms were selecting on the one signal they had, and it pointed the wrong way for the job they were filling.
