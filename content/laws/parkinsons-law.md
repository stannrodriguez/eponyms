## Origin

Cyril Northcote Parkinson, a naval historian, opened an unsigned essay in The Economist on 19 November 1955 with the sentence. The evidence was the Royal Navy's own returns: between 1914 and 1928 the fleet's capital ships fell by about two-thirds and its sailors by about a third, while Admiralty shore officials rose by 78 percent. He expanded the essay into Parkinson's Law: The Pursuit of Progress in 1958.

## Mechanism

A task has no natural stopping point. Quality is open-ended — there is always another draft, another check, another slide — so in the absence of an external limit the deadline is the only thing that decides when work ends. Parkinson identified the mechanism inside bureaucracies too: an official under pressure prefers subordinates to rivals, and subordinates generate work for each other, so headcount grows on a schedule unrelated to the work available.

## Where it breaks

It describes work with no external clock and no marginal cost to continuing. It does not describe metered work — a queue of support tickets or a production line is bounded by arrival rate, not by the hours available, and cutting the day does not cut the queue. The bureaucratic half was fitted to two Admiralty series and the growth rate Parkinson derived from them has never generalized. Shortening a deadline also does not produce proportional output; it changes what gets dropped, and the choice of what to drop is the real variable.

## Example

Microsoft Japan closed its offices every Friday in August 2019 and put 2,280 employees on a four-day week. Meetings were capped at thirty minutes and five participants. Sales per employee came out about 40 percent higher than the same month a year earlier, and paper printing fell 59 percent. The trial was uncontrolled and ran for one month, but the direction is the one Parkinson predicted: the work had been sized to the week, not to itself.
