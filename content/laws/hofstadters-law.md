## Origin

Douglas Hofstadter stated it in Gödel, Escher, Bach in 1979, in a passage about how badly early researchers had predicted when a computer would beat a human at chess. The self-reference is the point: the correction clause is included so that the law survives its own application.

## Mechanism

Estimates are built by decomposing the job into the steps you can currently name, and the steps you cannot name are exactly the ones that will cost the most. Delays are also correlated rather than independent — the thing that blocks one step usually blocks the next — so slippage compounds instead of averaging out. The recursive clause holds because applying a multiplier does not add the missing steps; it scales the same incomplete decomposition.

## Where it breaks

It is an observation about a method, not a planning number — it gives no magnitude, so it cannot be applied. It also only bites on novel work estimated by decomposition. Repeated work with a measured distribution does not suffer from it: a crew estimating its tenth identical roof is drawing on outcomes, not on a list of imagined steps. The working fix is to estimate from the record of comparable finished projects rather than from the plan.

## Example

The Sydney Opera House was costed at seven million Australian dollars with a completion date of 1963. It opened in 1973 for 102 million. The design was not fully engineered when construction started, the shell geometry had to be solved after the podium was already built, and each revision generated work nobody had listed.
