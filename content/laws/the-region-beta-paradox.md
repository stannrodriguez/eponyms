## Origin

Daniel Gilbert, Timothy Wilson and colleagues named it in a 2004 Psychological Science paper, The Peculiar Longevity of Things Not So Bad. The name comes from their illustration: a person who walks any trip under a mile and cycles anything longer arrives sooner from the far destination than the near one. The region beyond the threshold — region beta — is the one that gets treated.

## Mechanism

Repair is triggered by a threshold, not by a running total. Distress above the threshold recruits the responses that end it: people rationalize, leave, complain, seek treatment. Distress below it recruits nothing, because nothing about it demands action today. So the mild version, which is easier to fix, is the one that lasts — the total harm can exceed the severe case simply by not qualifying for attention.

## Where it breaks

It describes recovery processes that fire above a threshold, not every persistent annoyance. Plenty of mild problems last because fixing them genuinely is not worth the cost, which is correct behaviour rather than a paradox. The published effects concern affective recovery from discrete events and are modest in size; stretched into a general theory of tolerating bad situations, it outruns the evidence.

## Example

In the studies behind the paper, people asked to predict their reaction to an insult expected a severe one to sting far longer than a mild one. It did not. The severe version triggered the machinery that ends it — reinterpretation, dismissal of the source, telling someone — and faded quickly. The mild version was not bad enough to justify any of that, so it was still there days later.
