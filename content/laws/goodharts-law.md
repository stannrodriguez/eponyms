## Origin

Charles Goodhart was chief adviser at the Bank of England when he wrote the original in a 1975 paper for a Reserve Bank of Australia conference: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. He meant monetary aggregates — the relationships between money supply measures and inflation that had held while nobody was steering by them, and stopped holding once the Bank did. The compressed wording everyone quotes is Marilyn Strathern's, from a 1997 paper on British university audit.

## Mechanism

The correlation between a proxy and the thing you want was produced by a system that was not trying to move the proxy. Once it is a target, effort flows to whichever route raises the number most cheaply, and the cheapest route is almost never the one that also produces the goal. The measured quantity keeps moving; the relationship it was standing in for does not survive.

## Where it breaks

It is not an argument against measurement. The collapse comes from the pressure, not the counting — a metric that is watched but carries no reward stays informative, which is why instrumentation and targets should not be the same numbers. It is also weak where the proxy and the goal nearly coincide, and where gaming is cheaper to detect than to do. The law tells you a target will be met; it does not tell you the measurement was worthless.

## Example

England set an eight-minute target for ambulance response to the most urgent calls. The distribution of recorded response times developed a spike immediately before 7:59 and a cliff after 8:00 — a shape no physical process produces. Ambulances were dispatched to hit the clock rather than to match the case, and rapid-response vehicles were sent to stop the timer before a transporting vehicle arrived. The recorded times improved without demonstrating a corresponding improvement in patient outcomes.
