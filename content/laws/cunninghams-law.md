## Origin

Steven McGeady named the law after Ward Cunningham, recalling advice Cunningham gave him in the early 1980s. Cunningham did not coin the familiar wording and has since said the attribution garbles what he actually said, which concerned Usenet rather than the internet at large. His later creation of the first wiki helped make the idea feel characteristic of him, but it was not the source of the name.

## Mechanism

Answering a question is unpaid work with an uncertain audience. Correcting a visible error is cheap, has a target already written, and pays in status — the corrector gets to demonstrate expertise against a fixed reference point. A wrong answer converts an open-ended request into a concrete claim, and concrete claims attract responses that questions do not.

## Where it breaks

It requires an audience that already knows the answer and can be seen correcting it. Where the audience shares the error, a confidently wrong post produces agreement instead, and the error hardens. It also externalizes its cost: the wrong answer stays indexed and quotable long after the correction has scrolled away, so the tactic consumes the credibility of the venue it depends on.

## Example

An early study of Wikipedia edit histories found that obvious vandalism on well-trafficked articles was reverted in a median of under three minutes. The same mechanism fails without watchers: a hoax added to the biography of the journalist John Seigenthaler in May 2005, falsely linking him to the Kennedy assassinations, stood uncorrected for 132 days because nobody who knew better was reading that page.
