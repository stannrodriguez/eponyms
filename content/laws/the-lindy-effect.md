## Origin

Lindy's was a delicatessen on Broadway where comedians ate after shows and argued about how long a television comic had left. Albert Goldman wrote the argument up in The New Republic in 1964 as Lindy's Law, and got the direction wrong — he had total career length fixed, so every appearance shortened what remained. Benoit Mandelbrot corrected it in 1982 and Nassim Taleb made it a general rule in Antifragile in 2012: for the right class of things, age predicts more life, not less.

## Mechanism

It holds only for things that do not age — ideas, books, technologies, institutions — where survival is evidence rather than wear. For those, lifetimes tend to follow a power law with no characteristic scale, so the expected remaining life is proportional to the age already reached. Time works as a filter: a book still read after four hundred years has passed selection pressures you cannot enumerate and probably cannot even name.

## Where it breaks

It applies only to things that do not wear out. Applied to anything with a physical lifespan — a person, a machine, a body of infrastructure — it inverts and gives exactly the wrong answer. It also cannot distinguish survival from lock-in: an old standard may persist because switching is expensive, not because it is good. Kongō Gumi, a temple builder founded in 578, was the oldest company in the world for fourteen centuries and went under in 2006. Expected life is not guaranteed life.

## Example

Euclid's Elements has been in continuous use as a teaching text for roughly 2,300 years and is still in print. Every generation that could have dropped it had a reason to and did not. Nothing about the book's contents tells you it would last that long; its having lasted is the evidence.
