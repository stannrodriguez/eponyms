## Origin

Ian Betteridge, a British technology journalist, stated it in February 2009 while criticizing a TechCrunch article headlined with a question about whether Last.fm had handed listening data to the record industry. Andrew Marr had made the same observation in My Trade in 2004, describing the question-mark headline as a standard way of running a story you cannot stand up.

## Mechanism

A newsroom that had the evidence would write the assertion, because an assertion is a stronger headline and carries no risk when it is true. The question mark appears precisely when the reporting does not support the claim: it delivers the claim to the reader while leaving the outlet able to say it never made one. The construction is a marker of missing evidence, which is why it predicts the answer.

## Where it breaks

It covers a specific construction: a factual claim the outlet cannot stand up, delivered as a question. Genuine questions are not covered — explainers, interviews, and headlines posing a question the piece then answers. It also fails against a style guide that simply likes questions, and against a story where the affirmative answer is newly established and the question mark is doing nothing. It is a tell about editorial hedging, not a rule about punctuation.

## Example

The headline that prompted Betteridge asked whether Last.fm had handed user listening data to the recording industry. Last.fm denied it directly, its parent company denied it, no document or source ever surfaced, and nothing came of the story. The piece had a claim it could not support and a question mark that let it run the claim anyway.
