## Origin

Wallace Sayre taught political science at Columbia, and the line circulated among his colleagues through the 1950s and 1960s before anyone printed it. The version usually quoted is narrower: academic politics is the most vicious and bitter form of politics because the stakes are so low. Parkinson had described the same effect in 1957 as the law of triviality, with a committee approving a nuclear reactor in minutes and then arguing for an hour about a bicycle shed.

## Mechanism

Two things scale with the stakes, in opposite directions. High stakes bring in outside constraints — money, regulators, customers — that force a decision and cap how long anyone can fight. Low stakes bring none, so the conflict has no natural end. And where the material stake is near zero, the real stake is standing, which has no ceiling and which everyone present feels qualified to contest, because judging a bicycle shed requires no expertise.

## Where it breaks

Intensity sometimes tracks a stake that is not the nominal one. A fight over a name, a title or a seating chart can be a fight over authority, in which case the feeling is calibrated correctly and the law misreads it. The law is also the standard tool for dismissing a dispute the speaker has no stake in, which is a use it cannot support: it explains why low stakes permit long fights, not which fights deserve one.

## Example

Wikipedia's article on yogurt was the subject of formal move requests, mediation and tens of thousands of words of argument between 2008 and 2011, over whether the title should be spelled with or without an h. Editors invoked national varieties of English, dictionary precedence and prior consensus. The article's content was never in dispute.
