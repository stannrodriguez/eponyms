## Origin

The card above is a modern paraphrase of the principle. William of Ockham was a fourteenth-century English Franciscan and logician who used it constantly in the form plurality is not to be posited without necessity. The familiar wording entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity comes from John Punch, an Irish scholastic, in 1639. The idea predates both of them — Aristotle and Ptolemy state versions of it.

## Mechanism

Every independent assumption is another thing that has to be true, and the probability of a conjunction falls as terms are added. A theory with more free parameters can also fit more possible observations, including the noise in the data you happen to have, so it is easier to fit and less informative when it does. Both effects appear formally in Bayesian model comparison, where extra parameters spread a model's predictions thinner and cost it probability mass.

## Where it breaks

It breaks ties between hypotheses that explain the evidence equally well. It is not a preference for simple claims over complicated ones, and used that way it licenses ignoring evidence — some real mechanisms are genuinely intricate, and the razor never asserts that the world is simple. Simplicity also has no single definition outside a formal framework: counting entities, counting free parameters and counting description length can each pick a different winner.

## Example

Cholera in 1854 London was explained by miasma — bad air — which required a separate local cause for every cluster of deaths. John Snow proposed one entity instead: the Broad Street pump. It accounted for the shape of the outbreak, including the anomalies, since the nearby brewery whose workers drank beer had no deaths and the workhouse with its own well had almost none. Copernicus is the usual illustration and a bad one: his 1543 model still needed epicycles, was no more accurate than Ptolemy's, and did not win on simplicity until Kepler replaced the circles with ellipses.
