## Origin

The line was submitted by Robert J. Hanlon of Scranton, Pennsylvania, for Arthur Bloch's Murphy's Law Book Two in 1980, and it kept his name. Nearly the same thought is much older: Goethe has a version in The Sorrows of Young Werther in 1774, where misunderstandings and neglect are said to cause more harm in the world than trickery and malice do.

## Mechanism

It is a claim about base rates, not about human nature. In many everyday situations, fatigue, missing context, bad handoffs and ordinary error occur more often than deliberate harm. Starting with the more common explanation prevents suspicion from outrunning the evidence; it does not rule out malice when a pattern gives you reason to update.

## Where it breaks

It is a prior, not a verdict, and it stops working the moment errors stop being random. Mistakes that all happen to favour the same party are evidence, and applying the razor to them is a way of declining to update. At institutional scale the distinction thins out further: an organization that keeps making the same profitable error is not usefully described as incompetent, whatever is true of any individual inside it.

## Example

Wells Fargo employees opened as many as 3.5 million deposit and credit card accounts that customers had not authorized. Any single case looked like an overeager branch or a misunderstanding. The pattern did not: the accounts tracked a sales quota that required eight products per household, and the bank paid $185 million in the first 2016 settlement and fired thousands of employees. Errors that only ever run in one direction are not errors.
